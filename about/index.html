About

34 years producing evidence for people who carried the risk.

Lyle Holt spent 34 years making operational judgement calls under uncertainty, where getting them wrong had real consequences.

He had to work out what mattered, what was missing and what was being overlooked. He also had to spot what would disappear once a competing priority arrived. Over time he learned to notice what others had stopped seeing.

He brings the same habit to photography. Lyle photographs athletes, leaders and speakers, sporting organisations, and businesses and venues. Each shoot starts with where the photos will be used: a selection application, a LinkedIn profile, a sponsor deck, a tender or a board paper. He looks past the obvious photograph to the people and the work behind it, and thinks about the person who will eventually see the image.

For sporting organisations, he plans each frame around the questions a board, funder or sponsor is about to ask:

Can this programme represent us?
Can this programme justify our funding support?
Will this partnership stand up to board scrutiny when it comes up for renewal?

Operational Background

34 years in the Royal Australian Navy and Air Force

Lyle served 34 years in the Royal Australian Navy and Air Force across operational deployments, aviation, senior command and disruptive innovation.

He commanded international operations in Japan involving nine nations supporting the defence of the Korean Peninsula. He led more than 500 people operating Australia's air and space defence surveillance network. He flew the F-111 and built Number 5 Flight from scratch to establish Australia's medium-altitude, long-endurance uncrewed aircraft capability.

As Director of Plan Jericho, he questioned established assumptions and moved new ideas from concept into working capability.

What carries over to sport is the kind of environment he learned to work in.

Women’s pathways

His focus on women's elite sport comes from his military career. Again and again, it showed him the same problem from different angles. Did women have space to participate? Did the systems actually accommodate them? Was their contribution visible?

Most of the support that turns an athlete’s ability into recognition arrives once she reaches the top. Lyle works with women’s pathways to bring some of it forward, building the athlete’s and the programme’s identity while they’re still developing.

Why women’s elite sport →

Kabul, 2009. Here Lyle saw what access to sport made possible for a girl, and what its absence cost her. That thread runs from his military career to his work in women's elite sport today.

Heritage & Service

A family tradition of defending Country.

Lyle is a Palawa man. His father’s family comes from Flinders Island and traces its lineage to Mannalargenna, a Trawlwoolway leader and warrior of the Frontier Wars.

Lyle is the third generation of his family to serve. His grandfather Harold served at Gallipoli and on the Western Front, enlisted as a “natural born British subject” with his Aboriginality left off the record. His uncle Pat was a prisoner of war on the Sumatra railway, and later fought in Korea at the battles of Kapyong and Maryang-San. His father served aboard HMAS Anzac in Korea. Lyle pieced that history together only about a decade ago, through digitised family and mission records.

In his words to the Australian War Memorial: “The legacy is less about fighting foreign wars as it is defending country.”

Lyle’s Lens is a veteran-owned business based on Ngunnawal Country, in Canberra. Service and heritage are part of the public record, not a marketing line.
