Our Proving Ground

Why women's elite sport.

Women's elite sport in Australia is not an emerging market. It is an underbuilt one. The athlete quality is there. The funding pressure is there. The sponsor scrutiny is arriving. What is missing is the visual evidence.

Stronger Women. Stronger Communities.

Healthy, confident women contribute to healthier families, stronger communities and a stronger society.

Sport is one mechanism that can help create that — building health, confidence, connection and belonging.

But the pipeline leaks, and it leaks early.

The Participation Gap

Girls who start young stay. Girls who start late leave.

Registration records tracking 29,225 female participants over seven years found that 30 per cent of those who began a team sport between the ages of four and nine were still playing seven years later. For those who began between fifteen and nineteen, it was 4 per cent.1

The largest Australian study of why women and girls stop playing asked 5,055 of them directly. Girls aged 13 to 17 gave four reasons: it stopped being fun (34 per cent), they lost interest (32 per cent), an unfriendly coach or official (27 per cent), and injury (27 per cent).2

What kept them was social. Eighty-eight per cent of those who stayed had friends encouraging them, against 77 per cent of those who left. Among girls, 79 per cent of those retained enjoyed participating with family, against 47 per cent of those who did not.2

The elite end of the sport is drawing from a base that has already narrowed.

What Visibility Does, and What It Doesn't

Inspiration doesn't convert on its own.

The industry's standard claim is that visible women athletes bring girls into sport. Put plainly, the research doesn't support it.

An umbrella review published in February 2026, synthesising thirteen systematic reviews, found only limited evidence for an inherent demonstration effect — and concluded that any impact of elite sport on participation must be leveraged by other supporting activity.3

A sport can generate visibility and convert none of it. A programme can produce athletes worth knowing about and hold no asset that lets anyone know about them. Inspiration without infrastructure is a moment. Infrastructure is what turns it into something an organisation can use — in a recruitment campaign, a funding submission, a sponsor's activation, a board paper.

We build the infrastructure. We don't sell the moment.

The Commercial Case

The audience is smaller. It cares more.

Fewer Australians know a women's team than know a men's one — 31 per cent against 53. But among those who do, 54 per cent react positively to that team's sponsor, against 41 per cent for men's teams. More go on to consider or use that sponsor: 26 per cent against 19.4

While a sponsor of a women's team reaches fewer people, they get more from each person they reach.

Different businesses are buying into women's sport. Men's codes have long drawn beer, betting and automotive brands. The 2025 NRLW's top-tier partners were Harvey Norman, KFC and Westpac. The WNBL's most recent announcements included Bunnings, Sephora, Reebok and Blackmores. Supermarkets, banks, retailers. Suncorp said plainly why it backed netball: to reach families, and the women who make most household decisions.

Those businesses aren't buying a big crowd. They're buying association with athletes their customers like.

Men's sponsorship was built for the crowd — signage, logos, broadcast exposure. Decades of it. Women's programmes are asked to deliver commercial returns against the men's standard, without the same history, supporters or sponsors behind them.

Building a smaller version of it wouldn't help. A brand buying association needs photographs of the athletes that it can put in its own advertising. Most women's programmes don't hold any.
