The Gap We Work In

Why women's elite sport.

The next generation of women athletes shouldn't have to wait until they reach the elite level to look like they belong there.

The Pathway Gap

Value is created long before it's captured.

An emerging athlete is not only producing a performance outcome. She is building a pathway. Shaping the next generation behind her. Growing health and confidence — her own and others'. Representing an organisation before most people are watching. Creating community connection. Giving a future partner something to recognise early.

Established elite athletes have the infrastructure to build and manage their identity. Emerging athletes on the pathway often don't. Lyle's Lens brings that infrastructure earlier — while they're still building their identity, credibility and opportunity.

Built to Travel With Her

The same identity infrastructure moves with her as she rises.

Emerging — we build the identity. Established — we extend it beyond what the organisation's own campaign assets cover: who she is away from competition, what she represents, what she can carry into a boardroom or a sponsor deck. Post-elite — we translate it, as she moves from athlete to captain, coach, executive, ambassador, or board and industry figure.

The pathway is where we intervene earliest, because that's where the gap is greatest. It isn't the only place we work.

The Participation Gap

Half of all girls leave sport before they finish school.

Recent research puts the dropout rate for girls aged 15 and older at approximately 60 per cent, well above the 42 per cent recorded for boys at the same age.1 A 2019 national confidence study found that by the age of 17, half of all Australian girls had left sport entirely, despite two-thirds saying it made them feel more confident.2

The documented reasons girls leave are consistent: body image, social pressure, a competition structure built around boys, and a lack of visible role models. The first three are cultural. The last one is buildable.

The Role Model Effect

Girls stay when they can see someone to become.

Research by Visa and Year13 found that 60 per cent of young Australian women had no female sports star they looked up to.3 The domestic proof point is the AFLW: Roy Morgan recorded female participation in Australian Football growing by 21,000 in a single year after the league launched, reaching 176,000 players.4 Visibility moved participation.

The pattern holds internationally. A 2024 study in the United Kingdom found 66 per cent of girls attributed their interest in sport to female athletes.5 Research from the University of Toronto found 60 per cent of girls said they would stay in sport if there were more female role models, with role models reducing dropout by strengthening athletic identity.6 The mechanism is visibility. Visibility is what we build.

The Commercial Case

The fans are worth more. The infrastructure is missing.

The Fan Project, a research initiative led by Genius Sports and former Olympian Angela Ruggiero and presented at Axios Live during Cannes Lions, found that fans of women's sport, both men and women, watch for longer, spend more, and show three times the brand affinity of the average sports fan.7 The infrastructure required to attract and hold that investment is structurally different from traditional men's sponsorship models. It depends on athlete visibility, authentic identity, and deployable visual evidence that gives brands the confidence to commit.

Men's programmes at the same level have had decades to build their asset libraries, their media relationships, and their visual identity systems. Women's programmes are being held to the same commercial accountability by sponsors, funding bodies and boards, with a fraction of that foundation in place.

That gap is where Lyle's Lens operates.
