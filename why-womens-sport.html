The Gap We Work In

Why women's elite sport.

The next generation of women athletes shouldn't have to wait until they reach the elite level before they can hope for financial security.

Elite women athletes typically don't earn enough from their sport to sustain themselves. For the emerging generation, the gap is greater still — studying and working to survive while carrying the training, travel and competition load.

For many, staying on the pathway means finding another source of income. For others, it means relying on family support. Talent alone might not determine who can afford to stay.

The Pathway Gap

Value is being created before it is captured.

Emerging athletes create value well beyond the stadium.

They inspire the next generation. Build confidence. Represent their organisation. Create community connection. And become someone a future sponsor can recognise and support.

Established professional athletes have the infrastructure to build and manage their identity. Emerging athletes often don't.

Marketability is built alongside the athlete's performance and trajectory.

The U.S. college system understands this. Pathway athletes are recruited before they reach professional sport, with performance, imagery, profile and story working together to make potential visible.

The principle is simple: invest in the trajectory, not just where the athlete is now.

But the pathway is where the gap is greatest. That's where our framework begins. It isn't the only place we work.

Lyle's Lens brings that infrastructure into the pathway — where identity, credibility and opportunity are still being built.

Barriers to Participation

Half of all girls leave sport before they finish school.

Research puts dropout among girls aged 15+ at approximately 60%.1 By 17, half of Australian girls had left sport — despite two-thirds saying sport made them feel more confident.2

The reasons are consistent: body image, social pressure, structures built around boys and a lack of visible role models.

But there is another pressure further up the pathway.

If girls can see that even elite women struggle to build sustainable careers from sport, the pathway can look less like an opportunity and more like a sacrifice.

The first three barriers are cultural. The fourth is buildable — starting with pathway programmes.

Smashing Down Those Barriers

Girls stay when they can see someone to become.

60% of young Australian women report having no female sports star they look up to.3 International research similarly links female athletes with girls' interest in sport and their willingness to stay.5 6

National professional sports showcase powerful national heroes. But the athletes girls see most regularly are often much closer to home: the emerging stars in their own clubs, competing in senior leagues, representative and state teams.

The pathway athlete is often the most immediately visible role model.

Visibility changes what girls can see. What they can see changes what they believe is possible.

And visibility has an economic effect too. An athlete who is visible, recognisable and clearly represented is easier for a sponsor to identify, understand and support.

That support matters earlier in the pathway — when it could affect who stays, develops and progresses, and who leaves.

Visibility is what we build.

The Commercial Case

The audience is there. The infrastructure isn't.

The Fan Project found that fans of women's sport watch for longer, spend more and show three times the brand affinity of the average sports fan.7

Women's programmes are being measured against a commercial model built around men's sport, even though their audiences connect with the sport for different reasons and with different values.

Established men's programmes have decades of asset libraries, media relationships and visual infrastructure behind that model. Women's sport is building its commercial infrastructure much later.

Women's sport is already demonstrating significant growth. The opportunity is to invest according to its trajectory, rather than waiting for the economics to look like established men's sport.

And that investment cannot begin only when an athlete becomes elite.

It begins when her potential becomes visible.

Performance, imagery, profile and story working together make that potential easier to recognise — by organisations, audiences and future partners.

That is the gap Lyle's Lens operates in.
